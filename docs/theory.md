# Theory notes

A tour of the physics behind each pipeline stage, aimed at someone who knows
undergraduate quantum mechanics and special relativity but has not met collider
simulation before. Each section says what the stage models, why that modelling
step is necessary, and which knob in MaJETstik controls it.

---

## 0. The quantities we use

Collider physics uses a coordinate system adapted to the fact that the two
colliding partons carry unknown fractions of their protons' momenta, so the
collision's rest frame is unknown along the beam.

- **Transverse momentum** $p_T = \sqrt{p_x^2 + p_y^2}$ — momentum perpendicular
  to the beam. Unknown longitudinal boosts do not change it, which is why every
  interesting quantity is expressed in terms of $p_T$ rather than energy.
- **Pseudorapidity** $\eta = -\ln\tan(\theta/2)$, with $\theta$ the polar angle
  from the beam. Differences $\Delta\eta$ are invariant under boosts along the
  beam for massless particles, so $\eta$ behaves like a well-defined "angle".
  $\eta = 0$ is perpendicular to the beam; $|\eta| = 2.5$ is the edge of a
  typical tracking detector; $|\eta| \approx 5$ is as far forward as
  calorimeters reach.
- **Azimuth** $\phi$ — the angle around the beam axis.
- **Angular distance** $\Delta R = \sqrt{\Delta\eta^2 + \Delta\phi^2}$. This is
  *the* distance measure of collider physics; jet radii, isolation cones and
  object matching are all defined with it.
- **Missing transverse momentum** $\vec{p}_T^{\,\rm miss}$ — the negative vector
  sum of everything observed. Momentum conservation in the transverse plane is
  exact, so any imbalance means something invisible escaped: neutrinos here,
  possibly dark matter in a search.

## 1. Event generation: the hard scatter

**What happens physically.** Two protons collide. Protons are not elementary:
each is a bound state of quarks and gluons ("partons") sharing its momentum.
Almost all collisions are soft glancing blows, but occasionally two partons
scatter with large momentum transfer. That rare, large-$Q^2$ interaction is the
*hard scatter*, and it is the only part of the event that perturbative QCD can
compute exactly.

**How it is modelled.** The cross section factorises into a part describing
the proton and a part describing the partons:

$$\sigma = \sum_{i,j}\int \mathrm{d}x_1\,\mathrm{d}x_2\;
  f_i(x_1, Q^2)\, f_j(x_2, Q^2)\;\hat{\sigma}_{ij}(x_1 x_2 s)$$

- $f_i(x, Q^2)$ is a **parton distribution function**: the probability of
  finding parton type $i$ carrying momentum fraction $x$, probed at scale $Q^2$.
  PDFs are measured, not calculated, but their $Q^2$ evolution is predicted by
  the DGLAP equations.
- $\hat{\sigma}_{ij}$ is the **partonic cross section**, computed from Feynman
  diagrams. MaJETstik's default process is
  $q\bar{q} \to \gamma^*/Z \to e^+e^-$, the Drell–Yan process, at leading order.

**Why this process.** Drell–Yan is the standard candle of hadron colliders. The
final state is two isolated high-$p_T$ electrons whose invariant mass peaks at
the Z mass, 91.19 GeV — a value known to a part in $10^5$, so it calibrates the
detector. The jets in the event come for free from radiation (below).

**In MaJETstik.** `generators/cards/zee_jets.cmnd`, keys
`WeakSingleBoson:ffbar2gmZ`, `PhaseSpace:mHatMin`, `Beams:eCM`.

## 2. Parton shower

**The problem.** The fixed-order calculation above gives a Z and nothing else.
But the coloured partons entering and leaving the hard scatter radiate gluons,
and the probability of emitting a gluon that is *soft* (low energy) or
*collinear* (nearly parallel to its emitter) is enhanced by logarithms:

$$\mathrm{d}P \;\propto\; \alpha_s \,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta^2}{\theta^2}\,
   \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{E}$$

Both integrals diverge logarithmically at the edges. At LHC energies
$\alpha_s \ln^2 \sim 1$, so these terms are *not* small: truncating at fixed
order fails badly.

**The solution.** A **parton shower** resums them. It treats emission as a
Markov chain: starting from the hard scale, evolve downwards in a resolution
variable (Pythia uses $p_T$), at each step drawing the next emission from a
Sudakov form factor — the probability of *no* emission between two scales.
Iterating produces a cascade that turns each initial parton into many.

Two directions matter:

- **Final-state radiation (FSR)**: outgoing partons radiate, evolving forwards
  to lower scales. This gives jets their internal structure.
- **Initial-state radiation (ISR)**: incoming partons radiated *before* the
  collision. This is generated by *backward* evolution from the hard scatter up
  towards the proton. ISR is what turns "$Z \to ee$" into "$Z$ + jets" — the
  jets in the default sample are ISR jets.

> Backward evolution has a practical consequence in this codebase: ISR entries
> in Pythia's event record (status −41/−42) list mothers that appear *later* in
> the record. `majetstik/hepmc.py` topologically sorts the record before
> writing HepMC3 for exactly this reason.

**The underlying event.** The other partons in the two protons also interact
("multiparton interactions"), adding soft particles everywhere. This is not a
nuisance to be ignored — it shifts jet energies and is why grooming exists.

**In MaJETstik.** `PartonLevel:ISR`, `PartonLevel:FSR`, `PartonLevel:MPI`,
`Tune:pp`. Switch them off one at a time and watch the jet multiplicity change.

## 3. Hadronisation

**The problem.** QCD is confining: the potential between two colour charges
grows linearly with separation, $V(r) \approx \kappa r$ with
$\kappa \approx 1$ GeV/fm. Free quarks and gluons cannot reach a detector. But
at the confinement scale $\alpha_s \sim 1$, so this is non-perturbative — it
must be modelled, not calculated.

**The Lund string model** (what Pythia uses). Colour-connected partons are
joined by a relativistic string representing the colour flux tube. As they
separate, the string's potential energy grows until it is energetically
favourable to break by creating a $q\bar{q}$ pair from the vacuum. Each
fragment repeats the process until only on-shell hadrons remain. The
longitudinal momentum sharing follows the Lund symmetric fragmentation
function; transverse momentum comes from tunnelling. Unstable hadrons are then
decayed by the generator.

**Why it matters here.** Hadronisation is why we need jets at all. A quark
becomes a spray of tens of pions, kaons and photons; no detector sees the
quark. The jet algorithm in stage 3 is the operational definition that turns
the spray back into one object comparable with theory.

**In MaJETstik.** `HadronLevel:Hadronize`. Turning it off gives parton-level
"jets" — a good exercise for seeing how much of a jet's mass is hadronisation.

## 4. Detector simulation

**What a real detector does.** Particles from the collision traverse, in order:
a silicon tracker inside a strong solenoidal field (charged particles curve,
giving momentum from the curvature radius); an electromagnetic calorimeter
(electrons and photons shower and are absorbed); a hadronic calorimeter
(hadrons shower); and muon chambers outside. Neutrinos leave nothing.

**Full simulation vs. fast simulation.** Geant4 propagates every particle
through every detector element, simulating each interaction — accurate, and
minutes of CPU per event. **Delphes** instead applies the *net effect*
parametrically:

1. Propagate charged particles in the magnetic field.
2. Apply a tracking efficiency $\epsilon(p_T, \eta)$ and smear the momentum by
   the measured resolution.
3. Deposit energy in calorimeter cells of finite granularity, smeared by
   $\sigma_E/E = a/\sqrt{E} \oplus b$ — the stochastic term from shower
   sampling fluctuations, the constant term from calibration.
4. Run **particle flow**: combine tracks and calorimeter deposits into one
   non-double-counted list of candidates, using tracks where they are more
   precise (low $p_T$) and the calorimeter where it is (high energy, neutrals).
5. Identify electrons, muons, photons, taus and $b$-jets with parameterised
   efficiencies and fake rates; compute isolation.

The result reproduces the resolutions and efficiencies that drive most
phenomenology, at milliseconds per event. What it does *not* model is detailed
detector geometry, secondary interactions, or realistic pileup.

**In MaJETstik.** `simulation/cards/majetstik_cms.tcl`. The `ExecutionPath`
block at the top lists the modules in order; each module block configures one.

## 5. Jet clustering

**The definition problem.** "The spray of hadrons from a quark" is not
well-defined until you say precisely which particles belong to it. A usable
definition must be **infrared and collinear (IRC) safe**: adding an
infinitesimally soft particle, or splitting one particle into two collinear
ones, must not change the answer. Otherwise the perturbative calculation it is
compared against diverges.

**Sequential recombination.** All modern algorithms share one structure. For
every pair of objects compute

$$d_{ij} = \min\!\left(k_{T,i}^{2p},\, k_{T,j}^{2p}\right)
   \frac{\Delta R_{ij}^2}{R^2},
\qquad
d_{iB} = k_{T,i}^{2p}$$

Find the smallest. If it is $d_{ij}$, merge $i$ and $j$; if it is $d_{iB}$,
call $i$ a jet and remove it. Repeat until nothing is left. The exponent $p$
picks the algorithm:

| $p$ | Algorithm | Behaviour |
|-----|-----------|-----------|
| $+1$ | $k_T$ | soft particles cluster first; irregular, "fluffy" jets |
| $0$ | Cambridge/Aachen | purely angular; clustering history follows the shower, so it is the natural choice for substructure studies |
| $-1$ | **anti-$k_T$** | **hard** particles cluster first; jets grow outward from hard cores into near-perfect cones |

**Why anti-$k_T$ won.** Because the negative power makes hard particles
dominate, an anti-$k_T$ jet's boundary is set by its hard core and is almost
unaffected by soft radiation. The jets are regular and their area is
predictable, which makes them *calibratable*. It is IRC safe, unlike the older
cone algorithms it replaced. $R = 0.4$ is the LHC standard for quark and gluon
jets; $R = 0.8$–$1.0$ is used when a boosted heavy particle's decay products
must be captured in one jet.

**In MaJETstik.** `--algorithm`, `--jet-r`, `--jet-pt-min`; the C++ kernel is
in `majetstik/fastjet.py`.

## 6. Jet substructure

When a heavy particle (W, Z, H, top) is produced with $p_T \gg m$, its decay
products are collimated into a single large-radius jet. Substructure
observables ask what is *inside* a jet.

### N-subjettiness

$$\tau_N = \frac{1}{d_0}\sum_k p_{T,k}\,
   \min\!\left(\Delta R_{1,k},\, \dots,\, \Delta R_{N,k}\right),
\qquad d_0 = \sum_k p_{T,k} R$$

The sum runs over constituents $k$; $\Delta R_{n,k}$ is the distance to the
$n$-th subjet axis. $\tau_N$ is small when the radiation is well described by
$N$ subjets. The *ratios* are the useful taggers, because they cancel the
overall scale:

- $\tau_{21} = \tau_2/\tau_1$ — small for a two-pronged jet (W, Z, H → $q\bar q$).
- $\tau_{32} = \tau_3/\tau_2$ — small for a three-pronged jet (top → $bqq$).

The angular exponent $\beta$ (config `nsub_beta`, default 1) sets how strongly
wide-angle radiation is weighted.

### Soft drop grooming

Grooming removes contamination — underlying event, pileup, soft wide-angle
radiation — that inflates jet mass and varies with the environment. **Soft
drop** reclusters the jet with Cambridge/Aachen, then walks the clustering tree
backwards, at each branching testing

$$\frac{\min(p_{T,1}, p_{T,2})}{p_{T,1}+p_{T,2}}
   \;>\; z_{\rm cut}\left(\frac{\Delta R_{12}}{R}\right)^{\beta}$$

If it fails, the softer branch is dropped and the walk continues into the
harder one; if it passes, the jet is kept. With $\beta = 0$ this is the
modified Mass Drop Tagger. The groomed mass is much closer to the mass of
whatever produced the jet, and — importantly for theory comparison — its
distribution is calculable.

**In MaJETstik.** `nsub_beta`, `softdrop_beta`, `softdrop_zcut`; branches
`Jet_tau1/2/3` and `Jet_softdrop_mass`.

> With the default $R = 0.4$ jets from Z+jets, almost every jet is
> one-pronged and grooming has little to remove. That *is* the lesson:
> substructure is a large-radius, high-$p_T$ tool. Run
> `generators/cards/dijet.cmnd` with `--jet-r 0.8` to see it do real work.

## 7. NanoAOD

Not physics, but it shapes how physics gets done. CMS's formats form a ladder
from RAW (full detector readout) through AOD (reconstructed C++ objects) to
**NanoAOD**: a single flat `Events` tree whose branches are plain numbers or
variable-length arrays of plain numbers. No custom classes, no dictionaries, no
experiment framework — `uproot`, `ROOT`, `RDataFrame` or bare C++ can all read
it, at 1–2 kB per event instead of ~1 MB.

The convention is `<Collection>_<variable>`, with all branches sharing a prefix
having equal length within an event. That single rule is the whole format, and
it is why a NanoAOD-style file can be analysed on a laptop.

See [`output_format.md`](output_format.md) for MaJETstik's exact branch list.
